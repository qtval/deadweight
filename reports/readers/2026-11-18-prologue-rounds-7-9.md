## What changed in the voice pass and in rounds 7–9

- **The ford choice has a stake.** Aubric promises a room "with a door on it", and he does not pay people who crossed him the night before. Taking the bridle costs "We'll talk about that door another day". Every reader in all three rounds named "Say nothing. Tomorrow he pays you" as the choice that pulled at them. "It had not had a door before" was picked as a strongest line three times.
- **The bank scene explains the letter once**, in one paragraph, and the Marshal is no longer introduced there. It is still each round's most-named weak spot, but shorter each time.
- **The antagonist is named at dawn**: Marrow says "the Marshal", Isolde gives the name and motive, Wren remembers "Your squire reads. I should see to that." Renamed **Sayle** after round 7.
- **Marrow with the knife out is plain and fast**, with the blade on Wren's wrist and her hand shaking. If Wren refuses her story she notches Wren's ear, and at dawn she tells Isolde her version, which Isolde keeps "by her".
- **Isolde has no contractions**, except when Wren says the river took the letter.
- **Clarity fixes:** the escort is a day ahead in Stint; Stint is named in scene 1; the King is gone and the council signs; the cover story is said in one sentence ("took an arrow from robbers and has a fever of it"); "thief of the peace" reworded.

## Kept on purpose, against a reader's note

- "It would have been easier to hate him if he had forgotten", "closed on nothing", "count your fingers till later", "longer… than it had taken him to die", "I caught myself hoping": each was called stock or manufactured by the editor, and each was picked as a strongest line by another reader in the same rounds.
- The "*Marrow remembers…*" lines. Four readers said they break the voice or repeat the scene. They are the game showing that it remembers, which is a design rule. Worth the owner's eye: they could move to the journal, or be thinned to one per scene.
- The said-one-thing, thought-another device and the returning refrain: called borrowed technique (not borrowed lines) by the First Law reader. They are the core of the voice; the refrain is now used once.

## Still open, and why another polish round will not close it

Nine rounds, two different sets of fixes, the same two failing scores. The round 9 readers agree on the cause, and it is the method, not any one line:

1. **One clever author.** "All four speakers are equally dry and equally quick." Marrow and Isolde each got a rule and a new register in one scene; over a whole chapter the reader still hears Wren's wit in three mouths. The banter over the body ("He'll stink…", "excellent time") is the place it shows most, and it is also the lines earlier panels liked best.
2. **Every paragraph ends on a button.** The editor has said this in all nine rounds. Cutting the listed ones makes room for new ones, because the drafting habit is unchanged.
3. **Nobody is frightened of anybody.** Even with the ear and the knife: "I never thought anyone would actually get hurt."
4. **Decent choices are free, and selfish ones are signposted.** "The selfish options announce themselves in the label, so resisting them felt free." Temptation cannot reach 4 while the honest route costs one ear and a sour remark.
5. **Wren catches Wren.** "Abercrombie's narrators lie to themselves at length and the reader catches them; Wren catches Wren every time, in one tidy line."
6. Smaller, and specific: the elbow choice is cancelled two lines later when Marrow tells; "decide by dawn" changes nothing; Wren gives no reason for telling Isolde the river took the letter; nobody worries aloud that Aubric's own escort will see him tomorrow; the dawn scene is still where the new player's attention drops; the ballad verse on the end screen is flat.

## Proposal

Stop polishing the prologue. Write the opening scenes of chapter 1 to different drafting rules, and test those rules on the panel before writing the rest:

- A paragraph ends plainly by default. One turn per scene, at its end.
- In any exchange at most one person is witty. The others are slow, literal, frightened or wrong.
- Wren's excuses run long and are not caught. No one-line self-diagnosis.
- Every decent option names its price in the label, and the price is paid on the page before the scene ends.
- One person in each scene can hurt Wren and has a reason to.

If those rules move the First Law score in chapter 1, bring them back to the prologue as a rewrite, not an edit. If the owner likes the prologue as it is, he can pass it himself; the panel is a stand-in for him.
