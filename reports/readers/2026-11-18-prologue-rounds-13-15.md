## What changed

- **Wren's want: the promise.** Wren has written Aubric's letters for six years, in his hand. For four winters Wren has carried a fair copy of his promise (the room, the door, six years' wages), wanting only his name. A dead man signs nothing. A man with a fever can. So Wren wants the corpse "alive" more than anyone, for a reason nobody else knows.
- **A new last choice, on the road:** Isolde leaves her pen in the inkhorn. Sign his name now, or wait for Vellmouth, his seal and a witness. ("It wasn't forging. It was finishing his sentence.") Isolde sees the ink on Wren's thumb. The prologue now has 36,288 paths.
- **Isolde already knows** Wren writes the letters ("He never used a comma in his life until he had you"), so the one thing Wren meant to sell is gone.
- **Round 15: Isolde prices Wren's death.** She asks Marrow what it would cost if the squire had gone into the river too, and counts silver onto the breastplate with Marrow's knife under Wren's jaw. She stops at seven.
- Fixed from readers: "He's breathing" (now Marrow says "Come on" to the corpse); Marrow's unclear gesture; the ear logic; a reason for giving up the letter; the three papers named differently; fewer closing quips.

## What the readers said about the want

It worked. "Finishing his sentence" and the last line (he "signed a paper for me the day after he died") were named among the three strongest moments by five of the nine readers. Shabbiness and temptation, the two criteria that used to sit on the line, now pass with room on the selfish and mixed routes.

## Why the First Law score is still 3

The reason has moved. It is no longer "Wren wants nothing". Across the three B readers it is now about length and manner, which a twenty-minute prologue can only partly give:

1. **Wren knows themself too well.** Abercrombie's people believe their own excuses. Wren explains theirs.
2. **Everyone is quick and dry.** Nobody is slow, stupid or boring for a moment; every exchange ends on a capper. One reader said it reads nearer K.J. Parker.
3. **Compression.** Those books let a scene sprawl and then turn nasty. Isolde goes from "Wake him" to the whole plan in ten lines.
4. **Threat has no duration.** The knife beats are over in three lines.
5. **The war is a word.** One roofless mill.

Also still open, from all three A readers: the dawn planning scene is where a phone reader starts to skim; the surrender paragraph on the bank is a briefing; the stacked remember-lines in the wrapping scene read as bookkeeping. The editor, three rounds running: too many paragraphs end on a dry one-liner.

## Recommendation (for the owner; not acted on)

The nine criteria are the measure of the demo, and they have now passed five rounds out of six. The First Law reader has never given any draft more than 3.5 in fifteen rounds, on a single chapter read cold. Either accept the demo on the nine criteria, or change the rule to "B's score ≥ 3 and nothing lifted" for single chapters and keep ≥ 4 for the whole game. Points 1–5 above are better spent as rules for chapter 1 (longer scenes, one slow or wrong person per scene, a threat that lasts a page, the war seen) than as a sixteenth pass over the prologue.
