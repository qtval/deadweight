# Prologue — The Greywater


* * *

The Greywater was loud in the dark, fast and black and no deeper than my belt, and Sir Aubric Dane had decided to cross it drunk.

I had been his squire six years. He could not be talked out of it.

"By sunrise, Wren," he said, again. "They'll ring every bell in Stint for the Lantern. I'll not turn up at noon like a tinker with a cough."

"Yes, Sir Aubric," I said. Piss on your bells, I thought. My feet were wet.

The songs called him the Lantern. Three days back he had talked the hill towns out of a ten-year war in one afternoon, and they had sent him off with two skins of their wine. He had drunk most of both.

His escort had gone ahead to Stint, the next town, to get the ringers sober. That left him a squire, a guide, and a chronicler asleep in the cart, with wax in her ears against his singing. She was paid by the verse.

He sat his grey horse in full armor, polished for the bells by me.

A light moved on the far bank, under the alders. That was Marrow, the guide. She had waded over to test the bottom and shouted back three words, and the words were "Wait for morning."

"Nine days to the capital, Wren. The council signs before it goes home for the winter, and the war's done," he said. "I promised the hill towns no one hangs. The council hates that. They'll swallow it from me. They'd not swallow it from any other man alive."

He held the last of the wine down to me. "Drink, Wren. Tomorrow you're squire to the man who ended the war. You'll never pay for a cup again."

He had never once paid me, either. But he said it so warmly that I forgot to mind. You warmed your hands at him and didn't count your fingers till later.

**The game offers:**
- Drink with him
- **▶ Hand the skin back untouched**  (chosen)

I handed it back. "One of us should be able to find the road," I said.

"One of us is a horse," said Aubric, and slapped the animal's neck, and laughed until I was laughing as well.

He sat up straight. "I shall be modest. 'Good people, I did nothing. A little talking.' Then I look at my boots. Do I look at my boots, or is that too much?"

"The boots are good, Sir Aubric."

"Then they shout 'The Lantern!' and I wave. Like this." He waved, and nearly came off the horse.

"The third verse of my song doesn't scan, mind. I must have that mended before the bells. And you'll be paid, the day we ride in. And the room. A room in my house in the capital, with a door on it. Remind me of the door. Bring me that paper of yours in Stint and I'll sign it."

He had been promising me that room four years. It had not had a door before.

I had it written out. I wrote all his letters, in his hand, and four winters running I had made a fair copy of that promise. I could do his name better than he could. While he lived that was no use. He would only say he had never signed it.

He saw me shaking.

He pulled off his riding gloves, the fleece-lined ones, and dropped them into my hands. "Can't have you sneezing through my triumph. Your mother would never forgive me. How is her chest?"

My mother washed shirts for the army and had spoken to him once. He had asked after her chest every winter since. I put the gloves on.

He gathered the reins.

He was to sign my paper tomorrow. In six years I had not seen him sign a thing for anybody who had crossed him the night before.

**The game offers:**
- **▶ Grab his bridle. He'll hate you for it.**  (chosen)
- Shout for Marrow. Let her be the one he blames.
- Say nothing. Tomorrow he pays you.

I caught the bridle. The horse tossed its head. Aubric leaned down, breathing wine, and unpicked my fingers one at a time, humming.

"I talked ten years of war to death in an afternoon," he said. "I believe I can manage a stream. You're a good squire, Wren. But you are a squire." He had stopped humming. "We'll talk about that door another day."

Then he put his heels to the horse and rode into the Greywater, sitting very straight, in case anyone was watching.


* * *

The horse went in to its belly, snorting. Halfway over, it put a hoof in a hole.

It went down on one shoulder. Aubric went off sideways in sixty pounds of steel, and the river shut over him.

I waited for him to stand up. The river came no higher than his belt. Any moment he would stand up roaring and blame the horse, and I would spend tomorrow drying armor.

A hand came out of the water, bare and white, opened, closed on nothing, and went back in.

**The game offers:**
- **▶ Go into the river after him**  (chosen)
- Stay on the bank. Run for the rope.

I went in. My legs were numb before I had gone three steps. I went down on my hands and came up with a mouthful of river that tasted of mud and horse, and I didn't find him so much as fall on him.

He got a hand in my collar. He pulled me down onto his breastplate and held me under. I don't think he knew it was me.

I couldn't get his fingers open. My chest was going. So I hit him. I hit the Lantern in the face with my elbow, three times, four, and felt something in his nose give, and he let go.

I came up and breathed and was sick down my own front. By the time I had a strap in my fist and had started to pull, he had stopped helping.

It took longer to get him onto the stones than it had taken him to die.


* * *

He lay on his back on the gravel with the river running out of him. He smelled of wine and mud.

I shook him. I thumped his breastplate with both fists, then heaved him over on his side. A great deal of water came out of him, and most of the wine, and no breath.

"Get up," I said. "Get up, you stupid old bastard." I was crying. I was so cold my teeth hurt.

And under the crying I thought, I won't have to be up before dawn to arm him.

Then I sat back on my heels and did sums.

Six years' wages, which he had always been about to pay. His purse hung on his belt. He gave silver to beggars where people could see, and owed it everywhere else. If I left it, it would be in somebody else's coat by morning.

**The game offers:**
- Take your wages. Somebody will.
- **▶ Leave it on him.**  (chosen)

I let it hang. I am not a thief. I weighed it in my hand first, only to be sure of that.

My paper wanted his name, and he was past signing. I could sign it for him now, and who would say he hadn't? I put that thought down quickly. It would keep. He had been going to talk about it another day. Without him I was a laundress's child who could read.

Then there was the letter.

It was on the same belt, in an oilskin case: the surrender. The council at Vellmouth would never have let the hill towns off the rope for anyone but him. They would not do it for his corpse.

Out on the water, Marrow's light had turned.

The case was dry. Men had bowed to him for carrying it.

**The game offers:**
- Take it
- **▶ Leave it on his belt**  (chosen)

I took my hand off it. That was a knight's to carry, and I was glad of it. I pulled his cloak half over the case so she wouldn't see it straight off.


* * *

Marrow came up out of the river with her lantern, and stopped when the light reached him.

She put her ear to his mouth and kept it there, too long. "Come on," she said, to him.

Then she sat up. "That's my fee gone."

His nose was bent flat to one side and leaking blood. I had pulled his wet hair across it, and it would not stay.

She turned his face to the light with one finger. She looked at his nose, then at my elbow, then at me. "River do that?"

"He fell on the stones," I said.

"Course he did," said Marrow. She let his head go, and it rolled.

> *Marrow remembers the Lantern's broken nose.*

She crouched across the body from me. There was a short knife on her belt. I had watched her gut a hare with it at noon. Now it was in her hand.

She reached over him and took my wrist, and laid the flat of the blade on the inside of it, above the glove. She didn't press. Her hand was shaking worse than mine.

"Crown hero drowns at a ford," she said, all in one breath. "Guide that picked the ford used to fight for the hill towns. Who hangs? Guide hangs."

I didn't answer. I was the only other one who had seen it. I looked at the knife.

She got her breath. "So he didn't drown. Hill stragglers. Dark. An arrow, and his sword out. Stragglers hang for that. They're hanging anyway."

**The game offers:**
- Agree. An ambush, and nobody here hangs.
- **▶ Refuse, with her knife on your wrist**  (chosen)
- Tell her you'll decide by dawn

"He drowned," I said. "That's what happened."

Marrow came round the body quicker than I could stand. She took my collar in her fist, where he had, and pulled my face down to his.

"Look at him."

Then she cut me. She took the top of my left ear between her finger and thumb and put the knife through it, a notch, the way they mark a sheep. It was only hot, and then my neck was wet.

"Say drowned, to anybody. I say the squire broke his nose. My word, yours. You're the one looks like he fought you."

She let go. I stayed where she had put me, with my blood dripping on his breastplate.

Her eyes went to the oilskin case on his belt. "That dry?"

It was. She kept her hands on her knees.

"My people signed that paper. Hill towns. It gets where it's going." She looked at me then, not at the case. "Somebody's got to carry it who they'll let through a gate."


* * *

It got light without any sun in it. Mist stood on the water, and the first fly found him. Over the river stood a mill with no roof.

I had sat by him all night in wet clothes, holding my ear. It had stopped bleeding and started to beat. Marrow had the horse blanket round her own shoulders, and I had stopped shivering, which I knew was bad.

He had begun to smell, or I had.

Isolde Ferrant climbed down from the cart with ink on two fingers. She had slept through all of it.

She was the Crown's chronicler, and she had written most of his ballad before we set out.

She looked at the body. She sat down on the wet gravel in her good skirt, and got up again.

"Wake him," she said.

Marrow and I looked at each other.

"No. Don't— no." She did not finish it. "Did anyone else see?"

"Just us," said Marrow.

Isolde turned to me. "Wren. Tell me how he died."

**The game offers:**
- **▶ Tell her the truth: he drowned, drunk**  (chosen)
- Tell her Marrow's story: an ambush
- Tell her he died saving you

I told it straight: the wine, the armor, the hole in the ford. I made sure to put in everything I'd done to stop him.

**The game offers:**
- Tell her about your elbow
- **▶ Leave out your elbow**  (chosen)

I left out my elbow, and kept that arm behind me.

Isolde shut her eyes. "In a ford. Drunk. Nobody will ever hear that."

"There's another true one," said Marrow. "Squire broke his nose for him."

"Then that is the one I write down," said Isolde, "if you are ever a trouble to me."

Isolde looked at him. "Close his mouth. He looks surprised."

I pushed his jaw up. It came down again. I tied it shut with my neckcloth.

Isolde sat down on the cart step and held her satchel.

"Ride the paper south," said Marrow. "Leave him."

"The council will not sign it," said Isolde. "The hill towns only signed because he gave his word that nobody would hang. The council will only sign while he is alive to keep it. A dead man keeps no promises."

"Then they'd best sign before they hear."

"They sit until the end of the month. Nine days. Then they go home until spring, and in spring the war starts again. Marshal Sayle has run it for ten years and been paid for every one. He would pay a great deal to see under that canvas."

Isolde looked at the river for a while.

"Then he is not dead. He has a fever. He rides under the canvas to Vellmouth, and the council signs at the door of his sickroom."

"Three of us know," said Marrow.

"Three of us know," said Isolde. She looked at me as if I were a line she meant to cut. "Could you carry out his pot for eight days, Wren, and tell his own men he is sleeping? Well?"

**The game offers:**
- Say you'll do it
- Ask what's in it for you
- **▶ Tell her it's madness**  (chosen)

"It's madness," I said. Somebody ought to say so. "He's dead. Anyone who lifts that canvas—"

"That is quite right, and I am obliged to you," said Isolde. "So when they lift it, somebody must be under there nursing him, and it had better not be somebody who thinks it is madness." To Marrow she said, "The squire thinks it is madness. The squire will say so, in Stint, to someone."

"He'll stink by day three," said Marrow.

"Heroes do. We shall burn something."

"And the guide that picked the ford?"

"Was never at a ford in her life. We came round by the bridge."

"Bridge is two days east."

"Then we made excellent time." Isolde stood up. "His men will ask the squire, Marrow, and the squire has a face that answers before the mouth does. I have been paid for a ballad with a triumph at the end of it, and I have spent the money. A word."

They went down the bank together, out of hearing, and left me with him.


* * *

The mist lay on the gravel now. I sat by him, hands in my armpits.

Down the bank Isolde was talking and Marrow was not. I heard "three", and "river", and then Marrow said a number. Once she looked back at me, and then at the river. I had watched her look at fords like that all week.

It was all right. They needed me. Somebody had to carry the pot, and his men knew me. And Marrow had only taken an ear. I had done nothing. I had gone in after him. Nobody hangs a squire for doing as he's told.

Isolde's satchel lay open on the cart step, a yard from my hand.

**The game offers:**
- Read what she's written about him
- **▶ Stay where you are, by him**  (chosen)

I stayed where I was, and put my hand on his breastplate, because it was something to hold. It was cold all the way through.

They came back up the bank. Marrow had her knife out and was looking at it, not at me.

"The fewer of us who know, the cheaper this gets," Isolde said to her, quietly, as if I were the horse. "What would it cost, if the squire had gone into the river as well?"

Marrow came round behind me. I heard her boots on the gravel and didn't turn round, because turning round would make it true. Then she had my hair in her fist and my head pulled back, and the knife under my jaw. "Ten silver."

"Six."

"Ten. It's me does it. And I'm sick after."

Isolde began to count silver onto his breastplate. I could not get a breath to go in. I watched the coins. One. Two. Three. The fourth rolled off into the gravel, and she went down on her knees after it. The edge moved whenever Marrow breathed. My bladder went.

**The game offers:**
- Tell them you write his letters. You can sign for him.
- **▶ Tell them his men will come looking for you**  (chosen)
- Beg

"His men," I said, all at once. "In Stint. They'll ask where his squire is, they always ask me what he wants, they'll not ask you, they don't know you—"

"We shall say you ran," said Isolde.

"Squire runs, lads go looking," said Marrow, still holding my hair. "Lads find the river."

Isolde stopped counting. "What do they call you? His men."

"The wick," I said.

"Louder."

"The wick. The Lantern's wick." Marrow's fist was shaking in my hair. She was laughing.

"Then they would miss it," said Isolde, "and go looking, and look under the canvas first. So that is what the squire is for."

Isolde picked the coins up again. Marrow let go of my hair, and wiped her hand.

I stayed where I was. I was crying, and the two of them let me.

She would write his letters from the sickroom herself, she said; she wrote a fair hand. I wrote a better one. I kept that to myself. It was the one thing I had left to sell.

Marrow moved to spit, and I had my arm up over my face before I knew it. Neither of them looked at me.

"Say it for me, Wren. He has a fever."

"He has a fever," I said. She had me say it until she was satisfied, the last time with my hand on him.

"Now. The surrender," said Isolde. "Where is it?"

She knelt and unbuckled the case herself, and checked the seal.

**The game offers:**
- Let her take it
- **▶ Hold out your hand. "I'm his squire. I carry it."**  (chosen)

My voice went up at the end. Isolde looked at my hand. It wasn't steady. Then she looked at Marrow, who had a thumb in her belt beside the knife and was watching the case and not either of us.

Isolde put the case in my hand.

"Of course," she said. "His squire."


* * *

We wrapped him in the cart's canvas. It took all three of us to lift him. I took the head, so as to have him between me and Marrow.

I had to shift my grip twice for my elbow. He was heavier than he looked, and he leaked. My breeches had dried on me, and neither of them had said anything about them.

Before the canvas went over his hands, I saw my own. I was still wearing his gloves.

**The game offers:**
- **▶ Put the gloves back on his hands**  (chosen)
- Fold his hands on his sword, like the songs
- Keep them. He gave them to you.

I pulled them off and worked them onto his hands. His fingers had gone stiff. It took a while, and Marrow swore at me for every finger. He ought to go to his bells with his gloves on. Isolde stopped to watch me do it, and I took a little longer over the last one.

"Wouldn't lie for my neck," Marrow said. "Honest or stupid. Costs me the same. I walk behind you now."

> *Marrow remembers you wouldn't lie to save her neck.*

Marrow cut the purse off his belt with one pull of the knife, weighed it, and put it in her coat.

"You didn't want it. I've carried him a night. That's mine."

> *Marrow remembers you didn't take the purse when you could.*

When it was done, Marrow wiped her hands on her coat and went to see to the horse.


* * *

By full light we were on the road south to Stint and his bells, with the Lantern under canvas in the back of the cart.

Isolde drove, humming his first verse. I lay in the back under the canvas with him, coughing up the Greywater a little at a time. I did not hold his hand.

Marrow walked behind the cart, where she could see my back.

About noon she stopped over a lot of fresh hoofprints. "Marshal's riders. Same way as us." If one of them looked under that canvas, Sayle had his war back by supper.

Vellmouth was eight days off by cart. We had eight left.

Then Isolde went behind the hedge, and left her pen in the inkhorn on the tailboard.

I had my promise out of the pack and unfolded. The room, the door, six years' wages, to his squire Wren. It wanted his name.

He would have signed it. He had said so last night. And she would not leave a pen lying twice.

**The game offers:**
- Sign it now, in his hand
- **▶ Not here. In Vellmouth, with his seal and a witness**  (chosen)

I put it back. Not with her ink, and not on a cart. In Vellmouth, with his seal, and somebody respectable watching a sick man's hand move. I had waited four years. I could wait eight days.

Somewhere ahead they would be ringing the bells for him. I caught myself hoping he would enjoy it. I needed him in a good temper. He had signing to do.
