# Prologue — The Greywater


* * *

The Greywater was loud in the dark, fast and black and no deeper than my belt, and Sir Aubric Dane had decided to cross it drunk.

I had been his squire six years.

"By sunrise, Wren," he said, again. "They'll ring every bell in Stint for the Lantern. I'll not turn up at noon like a tinker with a cough."

"Yes, Sir Aubric," I said. Piss on your bells, I thought. My feet were wet.

The hill towns had sent him off with two skins of their wine. He had drunk most of both.

His escort had gone ahead to Stint, the next town, to get the ringers sober. That left him a squire, a guide, and a chronicler asleep in the cart, with wax in her ears against his singing. She was paid by the verse, and she had the last one written already: he died old, in a bed. He had wept at it and paid her double.

He sat his grey horse in full armor, polished for the bells by me. The horse steamed. The air off the river smelled of cold stone and rotten leaves.

A light moved on the far bank, under the alders. That was Marrow, the guide. She had waded over to test the bottom and shouted back three words: "Wait for morning."

"Ten years of war, Wren, and I talked the hill towns out of it in an afternoon." He slapped the oilskin case on his belt. "Their surrender. All it cost was my word that no one hangs."

He held the last of the wine down to me. "Drink, Wren. Tomorrow you're squire to the man who ended the war. You'll never pay for a cup again."

He had never once paid me, either. But he said it so warmly that I forgot to mind. You warmed your hands at him and didn't count your fingers till later.

- Hand the skin back untouched

I drank. It was sweet and strong, and I had not eaten since noon, and I felt it in my legs at once.

He watched me swallow. "There."

He sat up straight. "I shall be modest. 'Good people, I did nothing. A little talking.' Then I look at my boots. Do I look at my boots, or is that too much?"

"The boots are good, Sir Aubric."

"Then they shout 'The Lantern!' and I wave. Like this." He waved, and nearly came off the horse.

"The council wants forty of those hill men on a rope, you know. They'll sign my peace all the same, with the whole country singing about me. Nine days to the capital, before the council goes home for the winter. From any other man's hand they'd wipe their arses on it."

"The third verse of my song doesn't scan, mind. Have her mend it. And you'll be paid, the day we ride in. And the room. A room in my house in the capital, with a door on it. Remind me of the door. You wrote it all down, didn't you? Bring me the paper in Stint and I'll sign it."

He had been promising me that room four years. It had not had a door before.

I had it written out, four winters running, in a fair copy. I wrote all his letters, and did his name better than he could.

He saw me shaking.

He pulled off his riding gloves, the fleece-lined ones, and dropped them into my hands. "Can't have you sneezing through my triumph. Your mother would never forgive me. How is her chest?"

My mother washed shirts for the army and had spoken to him once. He had asked after her chest every winter since. I put the gloves on.

He gathered the reins.

He was to sign my paper tomorrow. In six years I had not seen him sign a thing for anybody who had crossed him the night before.

I said nothing. Corrected, he got twice as stubborn and three times as loud, and I was tired, and I wanted my bed.

"Good squire," he said.

Then he put his heels to the horse and rode into the Greywater, sitting very straight, in case anyone was watching.


* * *

The horse went in to its belly, snorting. Halfway over, it put a hoof in a hole.

It went down on one shoulder. Aubric went off sideways in sixty pounds of steel, and the river shut over him.

I waited for him to stand up. The river came no higher than his belt. Any moment he would stand up roaring and blame the horse, and I would spend tomorrow drying armor.

A hand came out of the water, bare and white, opened, closed on nothing, and went back in.

**The game offers:**
- Go into the river after him
- **▶ Stay on the bank. Run for the rope.**  (chosen)

There was a rope on the cart. I ran for it, and I was glad to have my back to the water.

I fell twice in the slop, the second time with my knee on a stone. I threw the rope from the bank and it fell short. I threw it again and it went over him and he didn't take it. I could hear him. It was a noise like a man trying to shout through a wet cloth, and his heels were going on the stones, and I stood on the bank coiling the rope a third time, neatly, the way he liked it coiled.

By the time I thought of the horse, the noise had stopped. I got a loop over the saddle as it floundered out, and it dragged him ashore by one stirrup, face down, with his mouth full of gravel.

It took longer to get him onto the stones than it had taken him to die.


* * *

He lay on his back on the gravel with the river running out of him. He smelled of wine and mud.

I shook him. I thumped his breastplate with both fists, then heaved him over on his side. A great deal of water came out of him, and most of the wine, and no breath.

"Get up," I said. "Get up, you stupid old bastard." I was crying. I was so cold my teeth hurt.

And under the crying I thought, I won't have to be up before dawn to arm him.

Then I sat back on my heels and did sums.

Six years' wages, which he had always been about to pay. His purse hung on his belt. He gave silver to beggars where people could see, and owed it everywhere else.

**The game offers:**
- **▶ Take your wages. Somebody will.**  (chosen)
- Leave it on him.

I pulled off the gloves he had given me to do it, and put them on again after. The purse didn't come to six weeks.

Then my paper, the one about the room. He was past signing it. But I could do his name, and now he could never say he hadn't. I left that thought where it lay. Without him I was a laundress's child who could read.

Then there was the letter.

It was still on his belt in its oilskin case: the surrender, for the council in the capital. And he was not going to carry it in.

Out on the water, Marrow's light had turned.

The case was dry. That was her people's paper in it, and she was coming with a knife on her belt.

- Leave it on his belt

The buckle was stiff with cold, and my hands were worse. I got it open and pushed the case inside my shirt, flat against my ribs. Then I did his buckle up again, so he would look right.


* * *

Marrow came up out of the river with her lantern, smelling of wet wool, and stopped when the light reached him.

She put her ear to his mouth and kept it there, too long. "Come on," she said, to him.

Then she sat up. "That's my fee gone. Saw you stood there. Never said a word to stop him."

She leaned in and sniffed me. "Two drunks. One river."

She crouched across the body from me. There was a short knife on her belt. I had watched her gut a hare with it at noon. Now it was in her hand.

She reached over him and took my wrist, and laid the flat of the blade on the inside of it, above the glove. She didn't press. Her hand was shaking worse than mine.

"Crown hero drowns at a ford," she said, in one breath. "Guide that picked the ford used to fight for the hill towns. Who hangs? Guide hangs."

I didn't answer. I was the only other one who had seen it. I looked at the knife.

She got her breath. "So he didn't drown. He was shot, in the dark, with his sword out. There's hill men still in the woods who wouldn't sign. Outlaws. They did it. The Crown hangs them when it catches them, peace or no peace."

**The game offers:**

"An ambush," I said. "I saw it."

The knife came off my wrist. She looked at it, and put it away.

"Good. You learn quicker than he did." She unstoppered her flask and passed it over the body. It was hot all the way down.

Her eyes went to his belt, and the empty buckle. "Where's his letter?"

**The game offers:**
- Show her you have it
- **▶ Say you haven't seen it**  (chosen)

"Haven't seen it," I said.

Marrow took hold of my shirt and opened it, not roughly, the way you'd open a sack to see what was in it. She looked at the oilskin. She did the lacing up again and patted it flat.

"Lie to the chronicler, that's free. Lie to me again, that's the letter and two fingers."


* * *

It got light without any sun in it. Mist stood on the water, and the first fly found him. Over the river stood a mill with no roof.

I had sat by him all night. Some time in the dark Marrow had thrown the horse blanket over me and called me an idiot for needing it.

He had begun to smell, or I had.

Isolde Ferrant climbed down from the cart with ink on two fingers. She had slept through all of it.

She was the Crown's chronicler, and she had written most of his ballad before we set out.

She looked at the body. She sat down on the wet gravel in her good skirt, and got up again.

"Wake him," she said.

Marrow and I looked at each other.

"No. Don't— no." She did not finish it. "Did anyone else see?"

"Just us," said Marrow.

Isolde turned to me. "Wren. Tell me how he died."

**The game offers:**
- Tell her the truth: he drowned, drunk
- Tell her Marrow's story: an ambush
- **▶ Tell her he died saving you**  (chosen)

"I fell in," I said. "He came in after me. He got me out. The river kept him."

He would have gone in for a dog, if there was a crowd. So it was nearly true. And it turned six years of carrying out his pot into something.

"Oh, you little shit," said Marrow, softly. "We had a story."

Isolde looked me over. I was mud to the knee and dry above it. She nodded anyway.

"That is the third verse," she said. "That is the one that did not scan."

Isolde looked at him. "Close his mouth. He looks surprised."

I pushed his jaw up. It came down again. I tied it shut with my neckcloth.

Isolde sat down on the cart step and held her satchel.

"Ride the paper south," said Marrow. "Leave him."

"The council will not sign it," said Isolde. "The hill towns only signed because he gave his word that nobody would hang. The council will only sign while he is alive to keep it. A dead man keeps no promises."

"Then they'd best sign before they hear."

"They sit until the end of the month. Nine days. Then they go home until spring, and in spring the war starts again. Marshal Sayle has run it for ten years and been paid for every one. He would pay a great deal to see under that canvas."

Isolde looked at the river for a while.

"Then he is not dead. He has a fever. He rides under the canvas to the capital, and the council signs at the door of his sickroom."

"Three of us know," said Marrow.

"Three of us know," said Isolde. She looked at me as if I were a line she meant to cut. "Could you carry out his pot for eight days, Wren, and tell his own men he is sleeping? Well?"

"What do I get?" I said. My teeth were knocking together.

"Hah," said Marrow.

Isolde tore a strip from the foot of a page, wrote on her knee, and blew on it. "A place in a household in the capital. Written down, this time."

She knew about the promise. He must have told her, the way he told everyone. I took the paper anyway, and I did not tell her about the one in my pack.

"The squire sold out for a strip of paper," Isolde said to Marrow, not to me. "Someone will offer him two."

"He'll stink by day three," said Marrow.

"Heroes do. We shall burn something."

"And the guide that picked the ford?"

"Was never at a ford in her life. We came round by the bridge."

"Bridge is two days east."

"Then we made excellent time." Isolde stood up. "His men will ask the squire, Marrow, and the squire has a face that answers before the mouth does. I have been paid for a ballad with a triumph at the end of it, and I have spent the money. A word."

They went down the bank together, out of hearing, and left me with him.


* * *

The mist lay on the gravel now. I sat by him, hands in my armpits.

Down the bank Isolde was talking and Marrow was not. I heard "three", and "river", and then Marrow said a number. Once she looked back at me, and then at the river. I had watched her look at fords like that all week.

It was all right. They needed me. Somebody had to carry the pot, and his men knew me. And Marrow had put the blanket over me. I had done nothing. I had stood on a bank. Nobody hangs a squire for doing as he's told.

Isolde's satchel lay open on the cart step, a yard from my hand.

**The game offers:**
- **▶ Read what she's written about him**  (chosen)
- Stay where you are, by him

I pulled out the top page. It was the ending, already written: the Lantern dying in a bed in Vellmouth, with bells. The squire was in it, weeping at the foot of the bed, a whole verse. She had drawn a line through the verse. In the margin, small, it said: squire — river? cheaper.

I put it back as it had been. My hands were not much use for it.

They came back up the bank. Marrow had her knife out and was looking at it, not at me.

"The fewer of us who know, the cheaper this gets," Isolde said to her, quietly, as if I were the horse. "What would it cost, if the squire had gone into the river as well?"

Marrow came round behind me. I heard her boots on the gravel and didn't turn round, because turning round would make it true. Then she had my hair in her fist and my head pulled back, and the knife under my jaw. "Ten silver."

"Six."

"Ten. It's me does it. And I'm sick after."

Isolde began to count silver onto his breastplate. I could not get a breath to go in. I watched the coins. One. Two. Three. The fourth rolled off into the gravel, and she went down on her knees after it. The edge moved whenever Marrow breathed. My bladder went.

"I write his letters," I said, all at once. "All of them, six years, I do his hand, I do his name. If he's sick they'll want a letter from him, the council, and I can write it, nobody else can, you can't, I've seen your hand—"

"Show me," said Isolde. She laid a page on his breastplate and put her pen in my hand. Marrow kept hold of my hair.

I wrote his name. My hand shook, and it came out like a child's.

"That is not his," said Isolde, and put a fifth coin down.

I wrote it again, with the big loop. I wrote it a third time without being asked.

Isolde looked at the page. "He never used a comma in his life until he had you. I did wonder. So that is what the squire is for."

Isolde picked the coins up again. Marrow let go of my hair, and wiped her hand.

I stayed where I was. I was crying, and the two of them let me.

Marrow moved to spit, and I had my arm up over my face before I knew it. Neither of them looked at me.

"He dies of a chill he took pulling you out of a river," said Isolde. "Some other river. You are in the song. Think of that."

I did think of it. I thought of the line through the verse.

"Now. The surrender," said Isolde. "Where is it?"

- Keep it. "I'll carry it. I was his squire."
- **▶ Tell her the river took it**  (chosen)

"The river," I said. "It must have come off in the water."

Isolde went to the body and looked at his belt herself. The buckle was undone, not torn. She looked at it, and then at the river, and not at me.

"Then we're carrying a dead man nine days," she said, "to tell the council the war's over, with nothing to show them. So we'll find it. Or something very like it. You'll write it."

Behind her, where only I could see, Marrow looked at my shirt and rubbed her finger and thumb together.


* * *

We wrapped him in the cart's canvas. It took all three of us to lift him. I took the head, so as to have him between me and Marrow.

I had to shift my grip twice for my knee. He was heavier than he looked, and he leaked. My breeches had dried on me, and neither of them had said anything about them.

Before the canvas went over his hands, I saw my own. I was still wearing his gloves.

**The game offers:**

- Fold his hands on his sword, like the songs
- **▶ Keep them. He gave them to you.**  (chosen)

I kept them. They had fleece inside, and he had given them to me when no one was watching, which was the only time he ever gave me anything. If that was my six years' pay, I was having it.

"Sold my story for a verse," Marrow said.

Marrow ran her thumb along his belt where the purse had hung, and looked at me. Then she unbuckled his spurs and put them in her coat.

"Fair's fair," she said.

> *Marrow remembers the purse was gone.*

Isolde, passing with the end of the rope, said only, "Fall in something before the next telling," and walked on.

> *Isolde remembers you were dry above the knee.*

When it was done, Marrow wiped her hands on her coat. "Rest of the road's double."


* * *

By full light we were on the road south to Stint and his bells, with the Lantern under canvas in the back of the cart.

Isolde sat by the body and wrote. I drove, with my bad knee stuck out straight. I kept my chin down. I could still feel where the edge had been.

Marrow walked ahead, where I could see her.

About noon she stopped over a lot of fresh hoofprints. "Marshal's riders. Same way as us." If one of them looked under that canvas, Sayle had his war back by supper.

Vellmouth was eight days off by cart. We had eight left.

Then Isolde went behind the hedge, and left her pen in the inkhorn on the tailboard.

I had my promise out of the pack and unfolded. The room, the door, six years' wages, to his squire Wren. It wanted his name.

He would have signed it. He had said so last night. And she would not leave a pen lying twice.

**The game offers:**
- **▶ Sign it now, in his hand**  (chosen)
- Not here. In Vellmouth, with his seal and a witness

I signed it on the tailboard, by his feet. Aubric Dane, with the big loop he made when he was pleased. It was the best one I ever did.

I heard her skirt in the hedge and folded it wet.

"You have ink on your thumb, Wren," said Isolde, climbing up.

> *Isolde remembers the ink on your thumb.*

Somewhere ahead they would be ringing the bells for him. I caught myself hoping he would enjoy it. He had signed a paper for me the day after he died, and it was drying against my ribs.
