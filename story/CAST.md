## The spine: six creditors of a dead man's word

Aubric never wrote a promise down. He said it warmly, in front of people, and meant it while he was saying it. Every person in the cast is owed something he promised aloud, and spends the game trying to get it **in writing** from somebody else.

| Who | What Aubric promised, aloud | What they now want written |
|---|---|---|
| Wren | Six years' wages, and a place in his household | A place, with their name on it |
| Marrow | "Nobody hangs." The hill towns signed on that word | A pardon with her name on it |
| Isolde | A triumph to end her ballad on | Her name on the ballad, and a post at court |
| Tobin | A new roof for the chapel at Saint Weir, promised with his hand on the altar | The body, which is worth more than the roof |
| Sayle | "I'll see it put right, Severin," ten years ago | The record corrected: who held the light |
| Hesper | Repayment, in every letter for ten years | The estate in her own name, and no more bills |

The letter everyone is carrying is the only promise of his that *was* written down, and it is the only one he kept.

## The lantern night (the old lie under the new one)

Ten years ago, in the first winter of the war, a Crown column was caught at night on a marsh causeway under hill bowmen. Somebody stood at the end of it with a lantern held up, for two hours, so the column could find the way off.

- **The song:** Sir Aubric Dane held the light.
- **What happened:** a captain of foot named Severin Sayle held it, and took an arrow through the right hand doing it. Aubric came in with the last company, took the lantern from him while a surgeon was at the hand, and was the one holding it when the sun came up and eight hundred men looked to see who had saved them. By noon Aubric believed it himself.
- **Who wrote it:** Isolde, then a junior copyist with the column. Her first draft had a captain in the third verse. Somebody paid forty silver to have him cut. The patch has never scanned. (Aubric, in the prologue: "The third verse of my song doesn't scan.")
- **Who paid:** Hesper, newly married, settling one more of her husband's bills without asking what it was for. She has not asked about a song since.

So the Lantern was a story before he was a corpse, and three of the people Wren must lie to already know it. This is revealed in pieces: Sayle's hand (ch1, seen; ch3, explained), the third verse (ch3), the forty silver (ch5).

## The bastard with a face

**Marshal Severin Sayle** is the person who profits if the surrender dies. Not "his man": him. Decided 2026-11-18, to keep the cast at six.

- **Why he profits:** he has commanded the army all ten years of the war and has friends on the council. Peace means the Crown's clerks come south to audit ten years of army accounts, and he has been paying unpaid soldiers by selling the army's stores. And it would be the Lantern's peace: the last verse of another man's song, again.
- **How he does it:** he does not need to destroy the letter. He needs Aubric known to be dead before the council signs. Then his friends call it waste paper, the war resumes in spring, and the audit waits.
- **Why Wren is afraid of him, in particular:** Wren has stood in front of him once. He looked at Wren and said to Aubric, "Your squire reads. I should see to that." Wren has never found out what seeing to it means. (This is now in the prologue, in the dawn scene, when Isolde first names him.)
- **In game text he is "Marshal Sayle" or "the Marshal".** The "Lord-" is dropped: "Lord Marshal" is a First Law title, and the do-not-copy list applies.
- **He is petty before he is grand.** He counts tent pegs. He has a man flogged for a short barrel and reads his letters through it. He writes with his left hand, badly, and hides the right in a glove. Art: the glove, the folding desk, the second chair. Never the face.

## Wren (narrator, player)

| | |
|---|---|
| **Want** | A place that is written down. Under that: to be in the song. Under that: a bed, and to be warm. |
| **Fear** | Going back to the laundry tubs. Being noticed by someone who can hang people. From the prologue on: Sayle, who said he would see to it. |
| **Secret** | Wren has written Aubric's letters for six years, in Aubric's hand, and made them better than he would have. That includes every letter home. Wren can sign his name more like him than he could drunk. Path secrets from the prologue sit on top of this: the elbow, or "let it rust". |
| **Breaking point** | Player-driven. The pressures are fixed: Wren cannot stand to be thanked for the thing they did wrong, and will give up almost anything for a promise on paper. |
| **Why Wren talks this way** | A short true sentence with a number in it is the one thing nobody has ever hit Wren for. |
| **Rhythm, aloud** | Eight words or fewer. A beat late: often answers the question before last. Says "Yes" first, then one fact, usually a number. Uses titles. |
| **Verbal habit** | Counts aloud, and corrects other people's numbers even when it is unwise ("Six years. Not five."). Offers to carry it. |
| **Breaks the pattern** | When frightened past sense, Wren talks too much, all at once, and is ashamed of it afterwards. Once a chapter at most. |
| **Humour** | Thought, never said. Aloud, Wren is the straight man. |
| **Never** | Swears aloud at a superior. Makes a joke aloud. Explains themself. |

Sample lines (aloud):
- "Yes, Sir Aubric. Both skins. I counted."
- "He's sleeping. He was asked for twice already."
- "I'll take it. Forty pounds. I've weighed it."

## Marrow

| | |
|---|---|
| **Want** | Not to hang. Then: a pardon with her name on it, so she can go home to the hill town she was born in. |
| **Fear** | Rope. She has seen what was done in the Hanging Wood, slowly, and she arranged it. And being recognised by anyone from her own side. |
| **Secret** | Last year she was the hill army's pathfinder. They had not paid her in two years and their own foragers had burned her sister's farm. She sold the Marshal's quartermaster the track round the back of the Hanging Wood for forty silver. That defeat is why the hill towns were ready to sign. By her arithmetic she ended the war, and nobody will ever thank her for it. Sayle knows, and it is his leash on her. Rask, the hill envoy, suspects. |
| **Breaking point** | She will sell a road, a story or a corpse. She will not stand and watch another hanging. When a rope comes out she runs or she kills, fast and ugly, and is sick afterwards. And she does not sell to the same buyer twice. |
| **Why she talks this way** | She was not paid for two years on promises. Now she says the cost out loud before anyone can promise her anything. |
| **Rhythm** | Two to seven words. Drops the "I" and the "it" ("Saw you against the water."). No subordinate clauses. Worst part first. |
| **Verbal habit** | Asks a question and answers it herself ("Who hangs?"). Puts a price or a count on it: pennies, days, miles, fingers. Calls people by their job: squire, the chronicler, the friar, the Marshal. Never says please or thank you. "Fair's fair" when she takes something (four uses in the whole game, no more). |
| **Humour** | Arithmetic. She prices things that should not have a price, and the sum is right. |
| **Breaks the pattern** | When she is lying, or frightened, the sentences get long and she stops counting. That is the tell, and Wren learns it in chapter 2. |
| **Never** | A sentence with "because" in it. A compliment without a number. Anybody's name. A saying about life: she prices this road and this day, never the world. At most one closing quip a scene. |

Sample lines:
- "Horse is lame. That's a day. You've got eight. Now seven."
- "You lied to her. Fine. Lie to me, it's a penny a mile more."
- "Rain. Road's gone by noon. Add a day."

## Isolde Ferrant

| | |
|---|---|
| **Want** | The ballad finished, sung at court, with her name on it, and the post of King's chronicler that comes after. She has spent the advance. |
| **Fear** | Being a footnote. Going back to copying other people's words by the page. Being known as the chronicler who was there when the Lantern drowned. |
| **Secret** | Two. (1) She made him: the lantern-night song is hers, with the captain cut out of it for forty silver. (2) She was awake at the ford. She came down from the cart with wet ink on two fingers. She heard the horse go down and stayed where she was, because a chronicler who watched it happen must write what she saw. Her first question was "Did anyone else see?" |
| **Breaking point** | She will change any fact. She will not have her name taken off the work, and she will not burn it. Pushed past that, she tells the truth: all of it, unarranged, once, to the worst possible person. |
| **Rhythm** | Whole sentences with the verb in the right place. No contractions. When Isolde says "don't" or "I'm", she is frightened, and it should be written as an event. |
| **Verbal habit** | Thanks you or agrees with you first; the blade is in the second half of the sentence. Corrects one word in what was just said ("Not hill men. Robbers."). Says "we" when she means "you". Talks about events as text: a line, a verse, what scans. Uses Wren's name only when taking something. |
| **Why she talks this way** | She is building the account as she speaks. Every speech leaves the listener with a line to say or a face to wear. |
| **Breaks the pattern** | Contractions, and a sentence she does not finish. |
| **Humour** | Courtesy, exactly applied. She accepts your objection and makes it your job. |
| **Never** | Swears. Raises her voice. Says "I want". |

Sample lines:
- "How good of you to mention the rain. We shall say he asked to feel it on his face."
- "That is very nearly true, and I am grateful. Say it again without the 'nearly'."
- "You have been so brave about the horse. Now be brave about walking."

## Brother Tobin Ask

| | |
|---|---|
| **Want** | Aubric's body under the altar at Saint Weir. A saint means pilgrims, pilgrims mean a roof, and eleven people sleep under the roof he has: cripples from both armies, and four children. |
| **Fear** | Winter. And that there is nothing in the hearth: that he has spent thirty years laying out the dead for nobody. |
| **Secret** | He knows the Lantern is dead within the hour of meeting the cart in Stint; he has washed more corpses than anyone in the game. He says nothing, and bills for it later. And he has made a saint before: the finger-bone at Saint Weir came from a drowned pikeman. In chapter 4 he arranges the miracle. Then something happens that he did not arrange, and he does not know what to do with that. The game never says which it was. |
| **Breaking point** | He will lie, forge a relic and stage a miracle. He will not see a body, living or dead, left by the road. If the corpse is to be burned or dumped, or a living person turned out, the gentle man steals the cart in the night. |
| **Why he talks this way** | He is taking stock of you: how long you will last, what you are hiding, what you would give. Every kindness is also a question. He has done it at so many bedsides that he no longer notices. |
| **Rhythm** | Long, soft sentences held together with "and". Slow. Repeats a small word twice at the start ("Sit, sit."; "There now."). |
| **Verbal habit** | Begins with your body: your foot, your cough, when you last ate. Ends on a question: about your comfort when he is giving, about the cart when he is taking. Not every speech; one in three at most ends that way, or it becomes a tic. Says "we" and "let us". Never says "I want"; the chapel has need. Makes up proverbs about ordinary things on the spot. Blesses what is broken. Calls Wren "child", "my friend". |
| **Humour** | Gentle, and at his own expense: his belly, his feet, his singing. He makes you smile in the sentence before he asks for something. |
| **Breaks the pattern** | When he wants the thing badly he goes short and plain, with no "and": "Give him to me." It should be frightening. |
| **Never** | More than one bread or hearth image in a scene. Hurries. Names a price. Says "dead"; he says "resting", "gone in", "laid by". |

Sample lines:
- "Sit, sit, the rain has been at you all day and you are no drier for standing up in it, and there is a corner of this cloak that is nearly clean. Are your feet wet through?"
- "You have not slept, I can see it in how you hold your head, and I have sat up with worse than whatever it is. Who is it in the cart?"
- "The poor horse, and he never complained, which is more than I can say for my knees. Shall we walk a little slower?"

## Marshal Severin Sayle

| | |
|---|---|
| **Want** | The official account: that he won the war. Under that: the third verse back. Under that: no audit. |
| **Fear** | Peace, and the clerks who come with it. Being laughed at. Bells. |
| **Secret** | He held the light. He has never once said so, because to say it is to ask. And he has sold the army's stores to pay the army, in his own hand, which is ten years of evidence in bad left-handed writing. |
| **Breaking point** | He is tidy and reasonable until somebody laughs at him, or until bells ring for the Lantern where he can hear them. Then he does something untidy himself, with the bad hand, in front of people, and is ashamed of the mess more than of the act. |
| **Why he talks this way** | Ten years ago the thing he did was not written down, and another man got it. Since then nothing is real to him until it is on a page with the hour and a witness. |
| **Rhythm** | Even, tired, complete. Sounds like dictation, because it usually is. |
| **Verbal habit** | Speaks about you to somebody else while you stand there: his clerk, the room, the dead man. Conditional and passive ("It would be a pity." "He will be found a chair."). Says how long he has ("I have until the hour"). Lists in threes. Asks for some small tidy thing in the middle of a threat: a second chair, the window shut, the page blotted. Calls Wren "the Lantern's squire"; uses the name once in the whole game, with the offer. |
| **Humour** | None on purpose. He states a horror as a problem of scheduling, and does not notice that it is funny. Nobody in the scene laughs. |
| **Breaks the pattern** | The offer in chapter 3: he sends the clerk out, says "you", and uses Wren's name. It is the most frightening line he has. |
| **Never** | Raises his voice. Insults anyone. Speaks to Wren directly, until the offer in chapter 3. Says "one" for "I". Makes the same dry remark twice. |

Sample lines:
- "It is raining on the Lantern's squire. Have him stood under something. I shall want him dry enough to be legible."
- "He says the horse is lame. Write that he says so. Write the hour. It would be a pity if he were to say something different tomorrow."
- "I am told he is not accustomed to lying. He will be given time to practise. Not a great deal of time. Shut that flap, would you."

## Hesper Dane

| | |
|---|---|
| **Want** | To be finished paying. Her father's brewery back in her own name, which the law gives a widow and does not give a wife. |
| **Fear** | That he is coming home alive, grateful, and expecting a triumph she will be billed for. Then, once she knows: that she will be made the Lantern's widow in public for the rest of her life, which is a second marriage to the same man. |
| **Secret** | She has known for years that the squire writes the letters. Six years ago they got better and began asking after the brewery. She has been answering Wren, not him, and has never said so. And she paid the forty silver for the song, without asking. |
| **Breaking point** | She will keep silent, for a price, and the price is the whole truth told to her once. She will not say a false thing and she will not weep to order. Asked to do either, she tells the court, or shuts her gate on all of you. |
| **Why she talks this way** | For ten years she has been sent warm words and bills. She has stopped accepting anything she cannot count. |
| **Breaks the pattern** | Once, in chapter 5, she asks a question she does not want the answer to, and does not ask it twice. |
| **Rhythm** | Short and whole. A question, a wait for the answer, then an instruction. No adjectives she could not weigh. |
| **Verbal habit** | Questions that need a number, a name or a date: "How much?" "Since when?" "Who else?" Will not accept a vague answer and asks again. Brewer's words for people: gone off, short measure, watered. Calls him "Aubric", never "the Lantern", never "my husband". Asks Wren's name. |
| **Humour** | Stock-taking. She says the unsayable as an item in a list and is faintly annoyed when it turns out to be funny. |
| **Never** | Softens it. Sings, hums or quotes. Lies. |

Sample lines:
- "How long has the horse been lame? Days, not 'a while'. Then it should have been sold at the first town. Who decided to keep it?"
- "You're wet. There's a fire in the kitchen and nobody in it. Go and stand there, and then tell me what you came to tell me."
- "Is that true? I'm not asking if it's what you were told to say. I'll ask once more, and then I'll believe whichever you pick."

## Sir Aubric Dane (dead; quoted and remembered)

Breaking point: none reached; he drowned first. Sound and wit are in `story/VOICE.md`. He appears after the prologue only in what people quote, what he owes, and how he smells. ## Telling them apart (the test of this sheet)

If a line could be moved to another mouth, rewrite it. The pairs that drift together, and what separates them:

| Pair | The difference |
|---|---|
| Marrow / Hesper (both blunt) | Marrow drops words, swears, prices it, and answers her own questions. Hesper speaks whole sentences, asks real questions and waits for the answer, then gives a household instruction. |
| Isolde / Sayle (both formal) | **The closest pair; all three test readers said so.** Isolde speaks to you, warmly, thanks you first, and leaves you with a line to say or a look to wear. Sayle speaks to somebody else about you, in passives, and fixes the hour and a witness. Isolde edits the record; Sayle files it. A formal line with neither mark belongs to nobody: rewrite it. |
| Isolde / Tobin (both kind) | Isolde's kindness comes first and the task after. Tobin starts with your feet, wanders, and ends on a question. Isolde never wanders. |
| Marrow / Wren (both short) | Wren agrees first and gives a fact. Marrow never agrees; she gives the cost. |
| Tobin / Aubric (both warm) | Aubric is warm about himself. Tobin is warm about you. |

## Using the voices without wearing them out

The test readers could name every speaker, and the editor among them still called Tobin and Marrow "a set of tics". So:

- One signature move per speech, not all of them.
- Every character says plain, dull things too. Not every line is on-model, and not every speech ends on a button.
- Each has one way of breaking their own pattern (above). Use it once, where it counts.
- The manner comes from what they are after in the scene. Write the want first and the habit second.
